# Working Backwards

A short brief (1-2 pages) used early in product thinking — before you've decided to build, before there's a PR-FAQ. It forces customer-first reasoning and exposes whether the value is real or imagined.

## When to use

- Earliest stage of a product idea
- Before committing to a PR-FAQ or a build
- When the team is debating "should we even do this"

## Structure

The whole document answers five questions in order. Prose, not bullets.

1. **Who is the customer?** Be specific. Not "users" or "homeowners." Narrow until you can picture one specific person in one specific situation. Example: "Suburban homeowners aged 35-55 who have just noticed a leak or visible damage in the last 90 days, and who have never replaced a roof before."

2. **What is the customer's problem or opportunity?** What pain are they in? In their words if possible — pulled from real research, support tickets, or interviews. Not imagined.

3. **What is the most important customer benefit?** One thing. The single biggest gain we'd deliver. If you list three, you don't know yet — narrow.

4. **How do we know what the customer needs?** Data, anecdotes, research. Not hunches. If the only evidence is "I think customers want X," go talk to a customer first and rewrite this section.

5. **What does the customer experience look like?** End-to-end, from the customer's point of view. What do they see, click, hear, feel — not what the system does. Resist the temptation to describe the implementation.

## Length

1-2 pages. Keep it tight. If you're writing more, you're probably ready to upgrade to a PR-FAQ.

## Failure modes specific to working backwards

- **Generic customer.** "Homeowners" tells you nothing. Narrow until you can name one specific person and one specific situation. If you can't picture them, neither can the team.
- **Imagined problems.** The "we know the customer needs this" section reads like a pitch. If your only evidence is intuition, that's the section that needs work — go research.
- **Solution sneaking in.** This format is about the customer, not your solution. If you find yourself describing screens, APIs, or features, you've drifted. Pull back to what the customer experiences.
- **Three benefits instead of one.** Hedging on the most important benefit means you don't know the answer yet. Pick one. The others can come later.
