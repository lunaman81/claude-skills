# 6-Pager (Narrative Memo)

The 6-pager is Amazon's primary format for decisions, strategy, business reviews, and analysis. It's 1-6 pages of prose, read silently in the first 20 minutes of a meeting, then debated. Bezos famously banned PowerPoint for major decisions in favor of this form.

## When to use

- Recommending a strategy or major decision
- Weekly or quarterly business review (WBR/QBR)
- Analysis of a problem or opportunity
- Anything you'd otherwise put in a slide deck

## Structure

There's no rigid template — the shape follows the argument. But most 6-pagers cover these elements in roughly this order:

1. **Thesis (1-2 sentences).** What you're recommending or what the doc concludes. State it in the opening line. The reader should know your position before they finish the first paragraph.

2. **Context.** What's the situation? What's changed? Why are we discussing this now? Frame the world the reader is walking into.

3. **The customer and the problem.** Who is affected and what pain are they in? Specific person in a specific situation. Use a real anecdote if you have one.

4. **The recommendation or decision.** What you're proposing, with numbers and dates. Specific enough that a reader could act on it.

5. **Why this and not the alternatives.** What did you consider? Why did you reject the alternatives? Don't strawman them — name the strongest version of each and explain why this one wins.

6. **Risks and tradeoffs.** What could go wrong? What are we giving up? What would have to be true for this to fail? The biggest risk should be visible, not buried.

7. **What we need.** The ask of the reader — approval, resources, a decision by a date. Make it concrete.

8. **Appendices (optional).** Data tables, supporting research, sources. Anything a careful reader would want to verify.

The body is prose. Headers help structure but don't replace argument. Use bullets sparingly — only for genuinely list-like content (budget items, options being compared side-by-side, a specific list of metros).

## Length

- **1-2 pages:** a quick decision memo
- **3-4 pages:** most strategy docs
- **6 pages:** only when the analysis genuinely needs it

If the doc is over 6 pages, the argument is unfocused. Cut.

## Failure modes specific to 6-pagers

- **Bullet-laden disguise.** A "narrative memo" that's 60% bullets isn't narrative. Convert to prose.
- **Slide-deck residue.** Single-line headers, lots of subheaders, "key takeaways" boxes. That's a deck pretending to be prose.
- **No tension.** The doc reads as if everyone agrees and the recommendation is obvious. If it's obvious, you don't need a 6-pager. Find the real tension and put it on the page.

## Example opening (strong)

> We recommend launching the Roofing wedge first, scoped to 30 cities, with a 90-day budget of 80 founder hours and roughly $2K in tooling. The customer is the homeowner who has searched "roof replacement cost in Dallas" on ChatGPT and gotten a national average from Angi instead of a real local quote. We can serve that homeowner with primary-source data no other site has — actual installer quotes from the last 30 days — and we have a 12-18 month window before Angi closes the gap. The biggest risk is launching before installer quote density is real in the launch metros; without that, we publish Angi-grade pages with an Openly logo and the moat collapses. The decision required from the CEO is whether to confirm quote density in 3-5 launch metros and approve the 80 hours.

That's the entire opening. In one paragraph the reader knows the recommendation, the customer, the moat, the biggest risk, and what's being asked. Everything after expands on this.
